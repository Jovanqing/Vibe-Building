# Shanghai Tower — elastic OpenSeesPy reproduction of Lu et al. (2011)

Source: 卢啸, 陆新征, 张万开, 叶列平. **特大地震下超高层建筑的倒塌模拟**.
中国科学: 技术科学, 2011, 41(11): 1405–1416.
(English version: Lu X, Lu X Z, Zhang W K, Ye L P. *Collapse simulation of a super
high-rise building subjected to extremely strong earthquakes.* Sci China Tech Sci,
2011, 54: 2549–2560.)

**This is a comparison of simulation against simulation. The paper reports no field
measurement.** Its nine periods (Table 2, printed page 1411) are the output of its own
nonlinear MSC.MARC model — layered shells for the core walls and the mega columns,
fibre beams for the frames and trusses, membrane elements for the slabs. The model in
this folder is elastic beam-column and truss elements with a rigid diaphragm per floor.
It answers a different question and none of what follows is a validation of either
model against reality.

Build: `python build_shanghai_tower.py [--viz]`, runnable from any working directory.
Numbers: `results.json`. Renders: `paper/`, `studio/`, each with `preview/` and
`index.html`. `--sweep` runs the mega-column orientation both ways. This report is
hand-written; the script writes `results.json` and the render sets.

## 3. The core: how the equivalent stick was computed

There are no shell elements here, so the core is one vertical
`ElasticTimoshenkoBeam` per storey on the core axis, which is also the rigid-diaphragm
master node. Section properties are integrated from the actual wall layout at the
mid-height of each storey, in `core_section()`:

1. **Wall layout in plan.** Outer wall: a 30 m square centreline with a `cut × cut`
   square removed from each corner, `cut` growing 0 → 9.0 m from the bottom of zone 5
   to the roof, which turns the square into the cruciform the text describes. Interior
   walls: two in each direction at x, y = ±5 m, i.e. the 3 × 3 cell pattern of
   Figure 6(a). Thicknesses interpolate linearly with height: outer 1.2 → 0.5 m,
   interior 0.9 → 0.5 m.
2. **A** = Σ tᵢLᵢ over every wall centreline segment. 252.0 m² at the base,
   120.2 m² just below the roof.
3. **I** — each segment is a rectangle L × t rotated into the global axes plus its
   parallel-axis term:
   `Ixx = tL³/12·sin²α + Lt³/12·cos²α + tL·y_c²`, and the mirror for `Iyy`.
   This is what makes the stick a *composite* section: a wide-column model with one
   stick per wall panel would need rigid arms at every floor to recover the same
   number, and would be an order of magnitude softer without them. 27 012 m⁴ at the
   base, 8 634 m⁴ just below the roof.
4. **Shear areas** Avx, Avy = Σ tᵢLᵢcos²α (resp. sin²α) — the web area parallel to the
   shear direction — fed to `ElasticTimoshenkoBeam` so the core carries shear
   deformation. 126 m² at the base. Leaving shear out would have made the core
   noticeably stiffer than it already is.
5. **J** — Bredt single-cell torsion on the *outer* wall loop only,
   `4A_m²/Σ(sᵢ/tᵢ)`, with the four face segments taken at the opening-reduced
   effective thickness of §5 and the corner-notch returns solid. The interior
   webs are ignored, which makes J a lower bound. As the corners are cut the
   enclosed area falls, the perimeter grows and the face walls shorten, so J
   drops from 26 317 m⁴ at the base to 4 611 m⁴ just below the roof: the
   cruciform core losing its closed-tube torsion, which is a real effect and is
   the mechanism behind the paper's own zone-5-to-7 failure narrative.

The outriggers reach the core through `rigidLink beam` constraints from the core stick
node to eight nodes on the core wall face at each mechanical level, so the outrigger
couple is transferred into the stick as a moment, exactly as it is in the real system.

Mega columns are `elasticBeamColumn` with the local strong axis radial, section tapering
5300 × 3700 → 2400 × 1900 mm, all properties multiplied by the transformed-section
factor 1 + (Es/Ec − 1)(ρs + ρr) = **1.3485**.

## 5. The core opening allowance — an assumption, not a fit

The intact Bredt value is an upper bound: a core with doors in every wall at
every storey does not act as a closed cell. Something has to stand in for the
openings, and the paper publishes nothing to build it from — it mentions them
once, in the collapse narrative on p.1411, to say they move from the middle of
the wall to its outer edge between zones 6 and 7, and never dimensions them.

**The stand-in is assumed geometry, fixed before looking at the paper's T3.**
Each of the four outer core face walls carries a group of lift and lobby doors
totalling **9.0 m** of its length (three 3.0 m doors), clear height **2.4 m**,
at every storey, capped so a door group never exceeds half the wall length.
Over the door height only (L − w) of the wall passes the closed-cell shear
flow, so one storey is two thicknesses in series and the face wall enters the
Bredt integral at

    t_eff / t = 1 / [ (1 − a) + a·L/(L − w) ],   a = h_door / h_storey

which gives t_eff/t = **0.812** at the base (L = 30 m, h = 4.45 m) and
**0.656** at the roof (L = 12 m after the corner cuts, h = 4.58 m). The
corner-notch return walls are taken as solid. Because the returns are a large
share of the cruciform perimeter, the resulting J comes out at 0.81 of the
intact cell at the base and 0.83 at the roof.

**The result that assumption produces:**

| | assumed openings | paper |
|---|---|---|
| 1st torsion | **2.3124 s** | 4.09 s |
| difference | **−43.5 %** | |

It misses by 43.5 %, and that is the result. With the assumed openings our mode
order is translation, translation, translation, translation, torsion — it does
**not** match Table 2's translation, translation, torsion. The sequence match
we obtained on an earlier pass was a product of the fitted value below, not of
the model, and is withdrawn.

### 5b. Inverse-inferred: what the paper's own core was effectively worth

Inverting our model against the paper's answer rather than predicting it: the
core torsion constant that reproduces T3 = 4.09 s exactly is

    J_eff = 0.22 × intact Bredt cell   (inverse-inferred, not used to build anything)

against the 0.82 our shear-flow opening model supports. The paper's layered
shell core is therefore effectively **about 3.7 times softer in torsion** than
that model allows. This says something about the original authors' model rather
than about ours, and it is supported by all three torsional modes
independently: our torsional errors under the shear-flow model are −43.5 %,
−46.7 % and −45.2 %, i.e. a single constant scaling of J explains all of them.
Whatever softens their core in torsion is uniform over the height and is not
the corner cutting.

**And the factor of 3.7 is not exotic — it is just openings.** On the
calibration curve of §5c, an effective torsion constant of 0.22 corresponds to
an opening area ratio of about **24 %**, i.e. roughly 10 m of opening per wall
per storey. That is large, but entirely constructible for a core with lift
lobbies on all four faces, which this one has. The shear-flow model of §5 was
simply the wrong physics for the job: it treats an opening as a local thinning
of the wall, when in torsion an opening interrupts the shear-flow loop itself
and costs far more than its own area. No exotic mechanism is needed, and the
open question in the previous version of this report is closed. (Curve source:
an independent reproduction in this project of a *different* building against
*measured* data — see §5c.)

## 5c. The differential opening rule, imported blind

A second reproduction in this project — a different building, a 280 m
frame-tube with a pierced concrete core in Singapore, checked against
**measured** frequencies, modelled with a shell core and explicitly meshed
openings — established two things that apply to anyone:

1. An intact meshed core sits at the closed-box limit, within 1 % of shear-flow
   theory. The element formulation is not the issue. Openings are.
2. **Openings do not degrade flexure and torsion equally.** At an opening area
   ratio of ~25 % per wall per storey the core keeps ~0.72 of its flexural
   stiffness but only ~0.20 of its torsional stiffness — a factor of about 3.5
   apart. The published curve of effective torsion constant against opening
   area ratio: 12.5 % → 0.56, 18.8 % → 0.34, 25 % → 0.20, 31 % → 0.13.

**Correction, recorded because it changed a result.** The first version of this
section used **0.85**, not 0.72, for the flexural side at 25 %. That 0.85 is
0.2174/0.2562, the ratio of the source model's fundamental *frequency* with and
without openings — a frequency ratio, not a stiffness ratio. Stiffness goes as
frequency squared, so the stiffness figure is 0.72, which is what the source
report states three lines further on. The torsion curve is unaffected: it comes
from a static twist test and was already a stiffness ratio. Everything below is
the corrected run; the superseded column is kept in §6 so the size of the error
is visible. One caveat on 0.72 that the source does not resolve: it is the
retained stiffness of that model *as a whole*, whose perimeter frame does not
change with the openings, so it understates what the core alone loses. It is
used as published.

That rule was applied here **blind**. The opening area ratio is taken from the
door geometry already assumed in §5 and the two dimensions the paper publishes:

    ratio = (9.0 m x 2.4 m) / (30.0 m x 580/124 m) = 21.6 / 140.3 = 15.4 %

Linear interpolation on the imported curve gives the **torsional** factor
**0.459**; linear interpolation between 1.00 at 0 % (result 1 above) and 0.72
at 25 % gives the **flexural** factor **0.828** (it was 0.908 off the 0.85).
These multiply the core stick's J and its Ixx/Iyy respectively. Shear area and
axial area are left alone — the rule covers J and I only. **Nothing in this run
is fitted to any period; all nine results below are predictions.** This is the
model that ships (`CORE_OPENING_MODEL = "differential"`); `"shearflow"`
reproduces the §5 column.

Re-deriving the inverse inference under the differential model leaves it
unchanged at J = 0.22 x intact Bredt, because softening the core in flexure
barely moves the torsional period.

translation, translation, **torsion**, translation, translation, **torsion**,
translation, translation, **torsion** — Table 2's order in all nine positions.
The corrected 0.828 lengthens the second translational pair to 3.0537 s, which
is now **1.8 % longer** than our first torsion at 2.9992 s, so the two swap and
torsion falls to mode 5:

| our mode | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | **5** | **6** | 7 | 8 | 9 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| T (s) | 9.7527 | 9.7527 | 3.0537 | 3.0537 | **2.9992** | **1.3572** | 1.3420 | 1.3420 | 0.8630 |
| character | transl. | transl. | transl. | transl. | **torsion** | **torsion** | transl. | transl. | torsion |
| Table 2 has | transl. | transl. | **torsion** | transl. | transl. | **torsion** | transl. | transl. | torsion |

Seven of the nine positions still match by mode character, but the first
torsional mode is in the wrong place, and the two torsional modes at positions
5 and 6 are adjacent where the paper has them separated by a translational pair.
The paper's first torsion sits 14.6 % *above* its second translational pair;
ours now sits 1.8 % below. **The better periods came with a worse ordering, and
that is the result.** §6 of the earlier version claimed the sequence match and
already warned that the 0.5 % margin made it fragile; the correction is what
broke it, from the other side.

Every period is still shorter than the paper's: the elastic stick-and-frame
substitute remains stiffer than the paper's layered-shell model in every mode.

A second honesty note: the paper's X and Y periods differ by 0.6 % (T1/T2) and
1.4 % (T4/T5). Our model is exactly doubly symmetric — mega columns in four
identical pairs, a square core, a rounded-triangle plan that only enters
through area — so its translational modes come out as exactly degenerate pairs.
That asymmetry cannot be reproduced from anything the paper publishes.

### Finding 1 — an elastic frame substitute for a wall core is progressively too stiff in higher modes

The error in the translational periods is not a constant offset. It grows
monotonically with mode number:

| | 1st translational pair | 2nd | 3rd |
|---|---|---|---|
| T ratio, §5 shear-flow model | 0.970 | 0.816 | 0.761 |
| implied stiffness ratio, 1/ratio² | 1.06 | 1.50 | 1.72 |
| T ratio, superseded I × 0.908 | 0.982 | 0.836 | 0.783 |
| implied stiffness ratio, 1/ratio² | 1.04 | 1.43 | 1.63 |
| T ratio, **shipped I × 0.828** | **0.992** | **0.855** | **0.804** |
| **implied stiffness ratio, 1/ratio²** | **1.02** | **1.37** | **1.55** |

About 2 % too stiff in the fundamental mode and about 55 % too stiff in the
third translational pair. Softening the core in flexure hits the higher modes
slightly harder than the fundamental, and the corrected, larger softening did
more of it — the stiffness excess fell by 0.02, 0.06 and 0.08 on top of what the
superseded factor had already taken off — but it barely dents the trend, which
survives a 17 % uniform reduction in core EI essentially intact. The trend is
therefore not an EI calibration error. We state this as a property of elastic
frame and stick substitutes for shear-wall cores rather than as a quirk of this
model: the substitute reproduces the global cantilever stiffness that governs
the fundamental mode reasonably well, because that is essentially one number (EI
at the base), but it has no mechanism for the local wall flexibility — pier and
spandrel deformation around openings, shear lag across the wall panels, in-plane
slab flexibility — that dominates the shorter-wavelength mode shapes. A second
reproduction in this project, an unrelated 280 m frame-tube building with a
pierced concrete core, independently shows the same direction and a similar
growth with mode number.

The practical reading: an elastic frame substitute for a wall core can be
trusted for the fundamental period to a few per cent, and should not be
trusted for higher-mode periods without an explicit softening allowance. That
matters for this building in particular, because the paper's own conclusion
(p.1411) is that its collapse is controlled by the **third** translational
mode — precisely the mode our substitute gets worst.

### Finding 3 — a core-opening rule derived from another building did transfer, on the periods

The differential opening rule of §5c came from a different building, a different
structural system, a different country and a comparison against measured rather
than simulated data, and it was applied here blind with an input taken from our
own assumed door geometry. **It transferred usefully on the periods.** All nine
improved against the §5 shear-flow model, the three torsional errors were cut
from 43–47 % to 27–31 %, and the fundamental pair went from −3.0 / −2.4 % to
−0.8 / −0.2 % — without a single quantity being tuned. That is a real result for
a rule with one prior application.

**It did not transfer on the mode ordering.** Under the corrected flexural
factor the first torsional mode falls to position 5 instead of 3. The
superseded factor put it in position 3, but that agreement was worth 0.5 % of
margin and rested on a mislabelled number, so it should never have been counted.

It did not close the torsional gap either, and the reason is instructive: what
failed to transfer is not the rule but our *input* to it. The rule maps opening
ratio to stiffness; §5b shows the paper's core behaves like a 24 % opening ratio
while our assumed doors give 15.4 %. Feed the rule the right geometry and it
lands on the paper's answer; feed it our guess at unpublished geometry and it
lands short by about the amount the guess is wrong. The transferable part — that
openings cost torsion several times more than they cost flexure, and roughly how
much — held on a building it was never derived from.

### Finding 2 — the mega-column orientation ambiguity is worth nothing

Figure 7 labels the 5300 mm mega-column dimension "y" but never says how that
maps onto the plan, so the section could be deep radially or deep tangentially.
Rather than choose, both were run (`--sweep`):

| mega-column 5300 mm axis | T1 (s) | T2 (s) | 2nd translation (s) | 1st torsion (s) | torsion falls at |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| radial (shipped) | 9.7527 | 9.7527 | 3.0537 | 2.9992 | mode 5 |
| tangential | 9.7529 | 9.7529 | 3.0539 | 2.9716 | mode 5 |
| difference | +0.002 % | +0.002 % | +0.007 % | −0.9 % | — |

For the periods themselves the ambiguity is worth nothing: the mega columns
carry the lateral system almost entirely by axial action into the outrigger and
belt trusses, so their own bending inertia — the only thing the orientation
changes — is negligible beside the core. Translational periods move by 0.007 %,
torsion by 0.9 %.

Under the superseded flexural factor this 0.9 % was enough to flip the mode
order, because torsion and the second translational pair sat 0.5 % apart. Under
the corrected factor they sit 1.8 % apart with torsion below, so both readings
now put torsion at mode 5 and the ambiguity changes nothing at all — including
the thing it used to change.

## 9. What the figures do not resolve

The eight zone plans in Figure 11 are perspective thumbnails about 120 px across in the
printed page. They resolve the plan family — a rounded triangle shrinking from base to
roof — the four dark mega-column pairs sitting on the faces of the core, the
disappearance of the corner columns above zone 5, and the progressive corner cutting
that turns the core into a cross. They do not resolve anything that needs a dimension.
The apparent rotation of the triangle from one zone to the next cannot be separated from
the viewing angle of each thumbnail, so we could not tell whether the *structural* floor
plate twists or only the facade does, and we built the columns without twist. Figure 6
shows the core wall layout as a shaded 3-D mesh rather than a dimensioned plan, so the
interior wall spacing, the depth of the corner cuts and — most consequentially — the
door openings are unmeasurable; the paper mentions the openings once, in the collapse
narrative on p.1411, and never dimensions them, and that single unpublished quantity is
what the assumed door geometry of §5 and the imported rule of §5c stand in for.
They get about half the torsional gap; §5b shows the paper's core behaves like
a 24 % opening ratio where our door assumption gives 15.4 %. Figure 7 gives one mega-column section at
an unstated elevation, with no taper schedule, no resolvable dimensions for the
encased steel, and no corner-column section at all. Nothing in the paper gives storey
numbering, floor-to-floor heights, zone elevations in metres, member sizes for the
outrigger, belt or perimeter framing, concrete grades, reinforcement outside the mega
column, or the foundation. Every one of those became a row in §8.

The generic instance is a plausible 632 m mega-frame, not this building: its mass comes
from a generic 6750 Pa caliber rather than the published total, it has no torsional mode
in its first six (its first six modes contain no torsion at all), and its first two periods
differ by 5 % where the paper's differ by 0.6 %. Reproducing the actual zone geometry,
the published mass and the core taper moved T1 from −12.8 % to −3.0 % of the paper's
value on the first pass and to −0.8 % on the shipped model, and put a torsional mode
into the first six — which the generic instance does not have anywhere — though at
2.9992 s in position 5 against the paper's 4.09 s in position 3. It did so with less
than a third of the nodes and less than a fifth of the elements, because the core is one
equivalent stick per storey instead of a braced cage and there is no facade grid. That
is a fair trade for a modal comparison and a bad one for anything that needs local
member forces.

## 11. Honest summary of what could not be reproduced

- The core wall opening layout — the single largest unknown, and the one thing the
  paper's own collapse narrative turns on. Our assumed door layout gives a 15.4 %
  opening ratio; the paper's core behaves like 24 %. The difference is a plain
  geometry unknown, not a missing mechanism.
- The torsional periods, still missed by 27-31 % across all three torsional
  modes after the differential rule.
- The mode ordering. Table 2 puts the first torsion at position 3; the shipped
  model puts it at position 5, 1.8 % below the second translational pair rather
  than 14.6 % above it (§6).
- The higher translational periods, missed by 13-20 % and getting worse with
  mode number (Finding 1).
- The X/Y asymmetry that gives the paper 9.83 vs 9.77 s.
- The material nonlinearity: the paper's "elastic" periods come from a model whose
  concrete constitutive law is already nonlinear at low strain, so even its own T1 is
  not a linear-elastic number in the sense ours is.
- Any member size in the outrigger, belt or perimeter framing.
- The slab membrane stiffness, replaced here by a rigid diaphragm — which is one of the
  reasons our higher modes are too stiff.
- The foundation and basement.
- The exact storey at which each zone begins, and hence which storeys are the
  mechanical ones.
